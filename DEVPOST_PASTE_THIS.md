## Inspiration

I make films with AI. Because of that, two kinds of people keep coming to me, and both of them are stuck in exactly the opposite way.

The first studied film. They know what a match cut is, they know why a low angle makes someone powerful, they can read a frame. And they are quietly terrified, because the tools they trained on are being replaced faster than they can learn the new ones. They open a list of forty AI video tools and have no idea which one to touch, what it costs, or whether the output is even legal to use in client work.

The second never studied film at all. They have an idea they genuinely care about. They open an AI video tool, type a sentence, get four seconds of something that looks nice and means nothing, and quit — because nobody ever taught them that a story needs a want and an obstacle, or that where you put the camera *is* the emotion.

Both of them want the same thing. Both of them are being left behind for opposite reasons. And the honest truth is that neither problem is solved by a better model. The first group needs the toolchain explained. The second group needs the craft explained. Almost every product I found teaches one and ignores the other.

**First Frame teaches both, in the same place, at the same time.**

I did not want to build a tool that makes films for people. I wanted to build the thing that stands next to you and explains what it is doing, so that after using it you can do more of it yourself. The person keeps the authorship. The system gives away the reasoning.

## What it does

You bring one idea. First Frame gives you back a film you could actually make — and teaches you why every choice in it works.

**1 · Concept Lab.** Your idea becomes a five-act blueprint: acts, shots, durations, camera and lighting direction, and an image prompt and video prompt for every shot. Generated by GPT-5.6 through the Responses API with a strict JSON schema, so the output is a structured plan rather than an essay.

**2 · The Creator's Room — the part I care about most.** The blueprint is then torn apart by a five-role AI crew: a Director, a Writer, a Cinematographer, an Editor, and a Test Audience. Each one criticises the plan from its own discipline, cites the specific craft principle it is invoking, proposes a concrete before/after revision, and the film is re-scored. You watch the score move and you can see exactly which change earned it.

> This is the difference between a generator and a teacher. A generator hands you an answer. A teacher shows you a draft, tells you what is weak about it, fixes it in front of you, and names the principle.

Watching a plan get *argued about* is how film students actually learn, and it is the closest thing to a real crew that a person working alone can have.

**3 · Film School.** 111 craft concepts — story structure, framing, lighting, colour, editing, sound — each with an in-depth explanation, an example of how it works in traditional filmmaking, and how to achieve the same thing with AI video, including honest caveats about where the AI approach breaks. Every citation anywhere in the product is a tappable link into this library, so the education is never a separate tab you have to remember to visit.

**4 · AI Tools.** 53 tools, catalogued with the questions people actually ask and nobody answers: what does it really cost, how long a clip can it make, can I use the output commercially, who owns it, and what happens when the tool shuts down. Unverified values are stored as `null` and displayed as "unverified — check official page" rather than guessed.

**5 · Footage Lab.** Local-only technical analysis of a video file — cut detection, sharpness, audio levels — computed entirely in the browser with canvas and WebAudio. Nothing is uploaded.

**6 · Showcase.** Real AI films by real credited creators, each shown with its recipe: the tool stack, the workflow in order, the prompts the creator actually published, and the craft concepts the work demonstrates. Watch it, then learn how it was done. Where a film was made with a tool that has since shut down, the card says so and links the current alternative.

**7 · Three tracks.** The same platform speaks differently to three audiences: a bright, one-decision-per-screen **Story Playground** for children that ends in a printable AI storybook; a **Weekend Film** track for adults who have never studied film; and **The Dark Room**, a deliberately dark, technical, timecode-and-waveform environment for film and media students filling the CG and VFX gaps they cannot shoot.

## How we built it

Every line of this was built with **Codex**, driven by **GPT-5.6**, and the product itself runs on GPT-5.6 in production.

**Two agentic GPT-5.6 passes.** `api/generate-story.js` produces the blueprint under a strict JSON schema. `api/creators-room.js` then feeds a compressed digest of that blueprint back to the model in a second, structurally different pass to critique it. The second pass is the product's actual differentiator, and it required a schema of its own (`lib/crew-schema.js`) and a specialist-lens system prompt (`lib/crew-prompt.js`).

**Hallucinated citations are impossible by construction.** Craft references are constrained to an allowlist of 111 IDs in `lib/craft-knowledge.js`. Any citation the model invents is dropped server-side before it ever reaches the page, and the drop is reported in the response metadata. This is not prompt-begging the model to behave — it is enforcement.

**The model cannot inflate its own grade.** Because the crew both critiques and re-scores, it has an obvious incentive to award itself a triumphant improvement. Score gain is capped at +30 server-side, and the system prompt states that a realistic gain is 6–18 points.

**The demo cannot break.** Every AI call has a deterministic offline fallback derived from the user's actual blueprint — the crew fallback finds the flattest adjacent tension pair, the most static climax shot, and whether the ending pays off the opening, then writes real revisions from those findings. When it runs, the page says plainly that it is a curated fallback rather than pretending to be live.

**No build step, no runtime dependencies.** 14 static HTML pages, vanilla JavaScript, Vercel serverless functions. It opens instantly and there is nothing to install.

**Verified continuously.** 6 Node test suites in CI assert the things that silently rot: that all 111 craft IDs are covered exactly once, that every tool reference resolves, that every showcase piece has a named creator and an HTTPS source, that user input is escaped before rendering, that unverified data is `null` rather than guessed, and — after a real bug where a stale cached script silently hid a whole section of the site — that every page loads shared assets at one consistent cache-busting version.

## Challenges we ran into

**Making an educational product honest was harder than making it impressive.** The easy version of this project fabricates: invented tool prices, plausible-sounding prompts attributed to films whose creators never published them, seeded comments, fake like counts. Every one of those would have made the demo look better. I chose `null`, empty arrays, and "prompts not published by the creator" instead, and then wrote tests to stop myself from backsliding. Ten of the twelve showcase films display no prompts, because their creators never released any. That empty space is the honest state of the world.

**A security bug found before launch.** A self-audit pass caught a DOM-XSS in the shot recommender where user input was rendered unescaped. It was fixed and a regression test now guards it, along with the same guarantee everywhere the Showcase renders user-supplied text.

**Deciding what not to build.** I wanted login, streaks, and an attendance calendar. I cut all three. Judges will not create accounts, streaks punish a tool people use in bursts, and a broken streak makes a product look abandoned. Progress is measured as capability — modules completed, skills exercised — and stored locally with the label "saved on this device."

**The hardest problem was the layer nobody asked for.** Analysing my own films frame by frame to derive cut counts and palettes was straightforward. Persuading myself not to fill in the tools and prompts I could not verify from the file alone was not.

## Accomplishments that we're proud of

**A model that argues with itself, and gets measurably better.** The Creator's Room is a working self-critique loop, not a demo trick. Five specialist roles produce grounded criticism, concrete before/after revisions, and a re-score the user can audit change by change. Building a system that grades its own work *and cannot cheat at it* was the hardest and most satisfying part.

**Education that is never a separate tab.** Every craft reference anywhere in the product opens the lesson behind it. 111 concepts, each written twice — once for traditional filmmaking, once for AI video, with honest notes on where the AI route fails.

**One product, three completely different faces.** A child, an adult beginner, and a film student open the same platform and meet three genuinely different environments — including a dark, technical, timecode-driven track that shares none of the main site's visual language. Designing for three audiences without building three products was a real design problem.

**Shipping something with zero dependencies that still does a lot.** No build step, no framework, no install. It loads instantly on a bad connection, which matters when your audience is students and children.

**Choosing honesty when nobody would have checked.** Empty prompt arrays, `null` prices, unverified fields labelled as unverified, an evaluation result marked as a single internal comparison rather than a benchmark. Then writing tests so future-me cannot quietly undo any of it.

## What we learned

That the gap is not capability. It is confidence. People do not fail to make things because the tools are too weak — the tools are astonishing. They fail because nobody ever told them what a good first decision looks like, and there is no faster way to feel left behind than to watch a technology arrive that everyone else seems to understand.

Also, concretely: that constraining a model with allowlists and schemas produces better output than asking it nicely; that a critique pass beats a longer generation pass; and that "I could not verify this" is a more valuable thing to display than a confident guess.

## What's next for First Frame

Finishing the maker-films layer — six of my own shorts are already analysed frame by frame and are waiting on the workflow and prompt details only I can supply, so that at least a few films in the Showcase carry a genuinely complete recipe. Shared reactions behind a real datastore. And more craft data extracted from more reference films, because the grounding gets better the more frames it has seen.

But the goal does not change. **Nobody who wants to make something should have to stand in front of an empty first frame alone.**
